AI-Generated Citation Failure: When Fluency Outruns Verification

Xufen Tu · Independent Interdisciplinary Researcher Public Citation Version: v1.0

A reference can look convincing before anyone knows whether it is real. In AI-assisted writing, a fluent paragraph may arrive with an author’s name, a publication date and a plausible title. Its polished appearance can make the supporting evidence seem settled.

The research problem begins when that appearance travels further than verification. A reference may point to no source, identify a real source inaccurately, or cite a source that does not support the claim beside it. Once the text is reused in another document, its form can acquire more apparent authority without acquiring better evidence.

The central observation is a difference in pace: text can be produced and circulated quickly, while checking what a source actually says still takes attention. Human involvement alone does not close that gap. A person may read and approve the wording while reasonably failing to notice that the reference does not support it.

My current judgment is that citation integrity concerns both evidence and responsibility. Readers need a traceable source for a claim, and the person or organization presenting the claim must remain answerable for it. Fluent language does not establish either.

This position does not claim that every AI-assisted sentence is unreliable, or that every citation error has the same consequences. The concern grows when an unverified claim enters research, professional advice, institutional records or other settings where people may rely on it.

The human responsibility boundary is therefore clear at the level of principle: the final published claim belongs to an accountable author or publisher. A generated reference cannot carry that responsibility on their behalf.
