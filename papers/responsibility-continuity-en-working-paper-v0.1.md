## Abstract

Artificial intelligence systems can generate, revise, translate, recombine, distribute, and operationalize information at a speed and scale that exceed the capacity of many traditional review and responsibility structures. Existing provenance, versioning, and file-integrity mechanisms can preserve the identity and history of technical objects, but they cannot by themselves preserve the human relation through which an output is interpreted, accepted, rejected, authorized, acted upon, and answered for. This paper introduces **Responsibility Continuity** as the maintained, visible, and interpretable connection among the information or version relied upon, the human judgment involved, the consequential decision made, the action that followed, and the person or institution that remains accountable for the result. The concept distinguishes technical continuity from responsibility continuity and explains why human presence alone does not constitute meaningful oversight. It argues that high-replication systems require identifiable human or human-institutional judgment nodes wherever uncertainty, value conflict, institutional authority, material impact, or irreversible consequence is present. The paper examines source, version, semantic, judgment, and responsibility breaks; clarifies the limits of provenance and file integrity; analyzes responsibility drift across distributed systems; and considers applications in research publishing, enterprise governance, public administration, education, professional services, software, and public archives. It does not offer a universal legal rule, an automated method for assigning responsibility, or a complete implementation architecture. Its claim is narrower: responsible AI depends not only on whether outputs can be traced, but on whether the relation through which information becomes judgment, judgment becomes decision, and decision becomes accountable action remains intact.

# 1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence is changing not only the speed of information production but also the structural conditions under which information becomes action. Text, recommendations, summaries, classifications, images, code, and operational instructions can now be generated, modified, translated, reformatted, and redistributed across multiple systems within a short period of time. An output may pass through several models, platforms, organizations, language environments, and human editors before entering a setting in which it produces material consequences. By the time a decision is made, the final user may no longer know which version was originally reviewed, which limitations were visible at the time, who interpreted the uncertainty, or who authorized the transition from information to action. The central governance problem is therefore no longer limited to whether an output can be traced to a source. It is whether the relations among source, version, interpretation, judgment, decision, action, and responsibility remain visible as replication increases.

This paper uses **high-replication AI systems** to describe environments in which information and AI-assisted outputs can be copied, transformed, recombined, translated, redistributed, and operationalized at a scale and speed that exceed the natural capacity of conventional review and responsibility arrangements. High replication does not refer only to identical copies. It includes semantic transformation, contextual removal, partial reuse, automated summarization, cross-domain adaptation, and conversion from informational content into organizational, technical, or institutional action. In such environments, content may survive while the relations required to understand and govern it disappear. A document can remain available while its original context is lost; a recommendation can circulate while the version on which it was based becomes uncertain; a decision can be implemented after the human judgment that authorized it has become impossible to identify.

Existing mechanisms preserve important parts of this environment. Hashes can indicate whether a file has changed; version identifiers can distinguish releases; timestamps can record events; repositories can preserve revision histories; and formal publication records can connect a work to an author declaration, date, and public location. W3C PROV similarly provides a domain-independent model for representing entities, activities, agents, and their provenance relations (W3C, 2013a, 2013b). These mechanisms are necessary because responsibility cannot be meaningfully reviewed when the relevant source or version has disappeared. Yet they are insufficient. A technically verifiable file does not reveal whether the person who relied on it understood its limitations. A publication record does not show why a later organization treated the work as suitable for a different context. A matching hash cannot establish that an action based on the file was justified. Provenance can preserve informational relations; it cannot independently create judgment, authority, legitimacy, or accountability.

The weakness of a purely technical account becomes most visible when AI outputs move into consequential systems. A model may generate a recommendation, but the recommendation acquires social or institutional force only when it is interpreted, accepted, rejected, modified, or acted upon. Between output and action lies a boundary at which uncertainty, competing values, institutional authority, and foreseeable consequences must be addressed. In routine and reversible settings, this boundary may be narrow. In high-consequence settings, it may affect rights, resources, professional treatment, institutional status, financial outcomes, public services, or irreversible impacts. The mere presence of a human somewhere in the workflow does not resolve the issue. A person may click an approval button without understanding the context, sign after the decision is effectively irreversible, or be held responsible for a direction embedded earlier in a model, policy, default setting, or organizational configuration. Elish’s account of “moral crumple zones” demonstrates how responsibility can collapse onto the nearest visible human even when that person had limited control over the automated system (Elish, 2019).

For this reason, the language of “human in the loop” is often too weak. It indicates that a human appears in a process but does not establish meaningful judgment, authority, or responsibility. A reviewer may be named without seeing the version actually used. An organization may require approval while making refusal impracticable. A final operator may be visible although the consequential direction was fixed upstream. Meaningful human control scholarship likewise stresses that human control must be connected to reasons, system behavior, authority, and the capacity to act, rather than reduced to nominal intervention (Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018; Siebert et al., 2022). The present paper therefore asks a stronger question: does a real relation remain between human judgment and the consequences that follow?

To address that question, this paper introduces **Responsibility Continuity**. Responsibility Continuity is the preservation of a visible and interpretable connection among a consequential decision, the human judgment involved, the information or version acted upon, the action that followed, and the person or institution that remains accountable as outputs are copied, modified, redistributed, translated, interpreted, and operationalized. It does not require one person to control every step of a complex system, nor does it reject automation in routine, reversible, and low-risk operations. It establishes a structural requirement: as automation, replication, organizational distribution, and technical complexity increase, consequential judgment and accountability must not become invisible.

Responsibility Continuity differs from technical continuity. Technical continuity asks whether an object, version, or system state can be identified over time. Responsibility Continuity asks whether that object remains connected to the judgment that gave it practical force. Technical continuity may show that a document remained unchanged; Responsibility Continuity asks who chose to rely on it, which limitations were considered, what decision followed, and who remains answerable for the result. The first concerns the persistence of information; the second concerns the persistence of accountable meaning and action.

The paper develops four questions. First, how can provenance remain connected to human judgment after information and AI-assisted outputs have been repeatedly copied, modified, redistributed, translated, and reinterpreted? Second, where must human judgment and responsibility remain visible when outputs move across models, systems, organizations, languages, jurisdictions, and versions? Third, how can file-integrity, version, and provenance records support accountability without being mistaken for proof of originality, legal ownership, factual truth, or decision correctness? Fourth, under what conditions must a human or accountable human institution remain identifiable as the principal judgment and responsibility node within an increasingly automated system?

The paper’s contribution is conceptual and structural. It distinguishes technical continuity from Responsibility Continuity; defines Responsibility Continuity as a relation linking version, interpretation, judgment, decision, action, and accountability; identifies human judgment as the connecting interface between informational outputs and consequential action; and explains why human presence alone is insufficient. The paper does not propose a universal legal rule, a complete software architecture, or an automated procedure for assigning responsibility. It does not assume that humans are always correct. Its narrower claim is that where decisions produce material consequences, responsibility must remain connected to a person or institution capable of understanding, contesting, correcting, and answering for those consequences.

# 2. Research Continuity and Conceptual Lineage

Responsibility Continuity did not emerge as an isolated label. It develops a research line concerned with the asymmetry between technological momentum and human judgment, the structural role of human judgment, the location of responsibility in high-replication systems, and the drift of responsibility across distributed AI-mediated processes. Earlier work on **Judgment Before Momentum** argued that the speed of generation, release, copying, and execution should not precede the judgment needed to establish meaning, boundaries, provenance, and responsibility (Tu, 2026d). Speed and legitimacy are not the same capability. A system may be able to produce and execute an option without possessing the authority to determine whether the option should be pursued.

Subsequent work developed human judgment as an **irreducible interface** in high-complexity systems (Tu, 2026a). The claim was not that every task requires direct human intervention or that humans are always superior to computational systems. It was that some decisions involve incomplete information, value conflict, institutional purpose, individual difference, and irreversible consequence in ways that cannot be reduced without remainder to another score, probability, or automated input. Human judgment in this account is not merely a temporary supplement to technical weakness. It is a structural interface through which information is placed into a context of meaning, authorization, exception, and responsibility.

The **Human Responsibility Node** extended that analysis by distinguishing human appearance from substantive human responsibility (Tu, 2026b). A person may participate without deciding, sign without understanding, or be designated as responsible without possessing the power to refuse, correct, or stop the system. A Human Responsibility Node is therefore not merely a natural person included in a flowchart. It is an identifiable position at which consequential interpretation, authorization, refusal, correction, or decision remains connected to a human actor or human institution with access to relevant information, effective authority, intervention capacity, and answerability for outcomes.

Work on **Responsibility Drift** then described how responsibility can become increasingly unclear as decisions pass through models, platforms, teams, contractors, institutions, and operators (Tu, 2026e). Responsibility may not disappear in a single moment. It moves. Developers may treat deployment as the user’s responsibility; users may rely on organizational policy; organizations may describe outputs as recommendations; final operators may be blamed for directions already embedded upstream. Nissenbaum’s earlier analysis of accountability in computerized society identified related barriers, including the problem of many hands, opacity, and the tendency to treat the computer as the source of action (Nissenbaum, 1996). More recent relational accounts likewise show that accountability is weakened when responsibility, authority, and the ability to answer are distributed without a coherent relation among them (Cooper et al., 2022).

The broader human-centered governance orientation of this research line was also articulated in *Primary Frequency System: A Structural Framework for Human-Centered AI Governance* (Tu, 2026c), which positioned human judgment, structural boundaries, and responsibility as organizing concerns rather than residual safeguards.

Responsibility Continuity joins these prior propositions. Judgment Before Momentum establishes that speed cannot substitute for judgment. Human judgment as an irreducible interface identifies the boundary at which information acquires meaning and direction. The Human Responsibility Node requires that consequential judgment remain connected to a real actor or institution. Responsibility Drift explains how this connection weakens across distributed systems. Provenance and version records preserve the informational basis upon which later review depends. Responsibility Continuity advances the combined claim that source, version, interpretation, judgment, decision, action, and accountability must remain connected across the entire movement from information to consequence.

This research continuity is established through public papers, dates, versions, DOI records, authorship metadata, and the author’s public research website. It does not depend on public access to private development repositories. Nor does the paper claim absolute global priority over every adjacent idea. Existing work has already developed data provenance, responsibility gaps, meaningful human control, reviewability, traceability, and algorithmic accountability (Buneman et al., 2001; Cobbe et al., 2021; Kroll et al., 2017; Matthias, 2004; Santoni de Sio & van den Hoven, 2018). The specific claim made here is that Responsibility Continuity names and defines a distinct relation: the continued connection of provenance and version to human interpretation, judgment, consequential decision, action, and accountable response under conditions of high replication.


# 3. Related Literature and Conceptual Distinction

Responsibility Continuity enters an existing field rather than replacing it. Provenance research, data lineage, traceability, explainability, meaningful human control, responsibility-gap analysis, algorithmic accountability, reviewability, and socio-technical governance each address part of the problem. Their differences matter because the central claim of this paper depends on identifying a relation that is not exhausted by any one of them. The purpose of this section is therefore not to present an encyclopedic literature review, but to locate the concept in relation to adjacent ideas and to clarify the specific theoretical work it is intended to perform.

## 3.1 Provenance and data lineage

Provenance concerns the history of an entity: where it came from, which activities affected it, and which agents were involved in producing or modifying it. W3C PROV formalizes these relations through entities, activities, and agents, allowing provenance information to be represented and exchanged across systems (W3C, 2013a, 2013b). Database research similarly developed data provenance to answer questions such as why a result exists and where particular data originated (Buneman et al., 2001). These traditions provide an essential informational foundation. They allow later users to distinguish source from derivative, identify transformation, and reconstruct the lineage of an object.

Responsibility Continuity relies on provenance but asks a different question. Provenance can show that a report was generated from a dataset, modified by a service, and delivered to an organization. It cannot by itself show how the report was interpreted, whether a human understood its limitations, who accepted the risk of using it, who possessed authority to act, or who remains obligated to correct the outcome. An entity–activity–agent graph may identify participation without establishing substantive judgment. A person may appear as an agent because the person uploaded, approved, or transmitted a file, while the consequential judgment occurred elsewhere or did not occur at all.

Data lineage is therefore necessary but not sufficient. It preserves informational dependence; Responsibility Continuity preserves the relation through which informational dependence becomes practical authority and accountable action. The distinction is especially important when the same informational lineage supports different decisions. Two institutions may rely on the same model output but interpret it under different legal duties, professional standards, or risk tolerances. The provenance is shared; the judgment and responsibility are not.

## 3.2 Traceability

Traceability is often used to describe the ability to follow requirements, data, models, decisions, or system components across a lifecycle. It can connect a deployed outcome to design documents, test results, model versions, and operational events. Kroll (2021) argues that traceability can operationalize accountability by making system processes and decisions more reconstructable. This is close to Responsibility Continuity, but the concepts remain distinct.

Traceability asks whether a path can be followed. Responsibility Continuity asks whether the path retains a meaningful relation among version, interpretation, authority, decision, action, and answerability. A system can be highly traceable while still distributing authority in a way that leaves no one capable of correction. It can record every click while failing to establish whether the clicking person understood the information or could refuse. It can reconstruct a sequence without reconstructing the normative relation that made the sequence legitimate or illegitimate.

Responsibility Continuity therefore treats traceability as an evidentiary capacity. Traceability makes review possible; it does not determine the content of responsibility. It tells us where to look. Responsibility Continuity asks what must remain connected when we look there.

## 3.3 Explainability and explanation

Explainability focuses on making model behavior or outputs understandable. Depending on context, it may refer to feature importance, local explanations, global behavior, counterfactual reasoning, causal accounts, or user-facing reasons. These capacities can support review, contestation, and informed use. Yet an explanation is not identical to the actual basis of a decision, and a fluent explanation is not evidence that judgment occurred.

In generative systems, explanation-like language can be produced after the fact, even when it does not correspond to the real causal pathway of the output. An organization may also provide a technically accurate model explanation while concealing the institutional decision that made the output consequential. A risk score may be explainable, yet the choice to use that score for exclusion, prioritization, or discipline remains a separate judgment. Responsibility Continuity therefore connects explanation to the actual version, decision context, authority, and consequence. It is concerned not only with whether a system can produce reasons, but with whether the reasons considered by the responsible subject can be distinguished from post hoc rationalization.

## 3.4 Human oversight and meaningful human control

Human oversight is now a central governance requirement. The EU AI Act requires high-risk systems to be designed so they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during use and describes capacities that include understanding limitations, avoiding over-reliance, interpreting outputs, overriding or disregarding them, and interrupting system operation where appropriate (European Union, 2024). These requirements reflect the inadequacy of merely placing a human in a workflow.

Meaningful human control provides a deeper philosophical account. Santoni de Sio and van den Hoven (2018) connect control to reasons and responsibility rather than to direct physical intervention. Siebert et al. (2022) further identify actionable properties, including the requirement that attributed responsibility be commensurate with a human’s ability and authority to influence the system and that explicit links exist between AI actions and responsible human actions. Responsibility Continuity is compatible with these accounts but extends the temporal and informational frame.

Meaningful human control primarily asks whether human agency and moral responsibility remain appropriately connected to system behavior. Responsibility Continuity asks whether this connection remains intelligible across source, version change, translation, reinterpretation, distributed authorization, action, and later consequence. A system might be under meaningful human control at deployment yet lose responsibility continuity after a model update, organizational transfer, language transformation, or personnel change. Conversely, a later reviewer may have authority but lack the version and decision context required to exercise responsible control. Responsibility Continuity therefore treats meaningful control not as a one-time property but as a relation that must survive transformation and institutional time.

## 3.5 Responsibility gaps and moral crumple zones

The responsibility-gap literature addresses situations in which autonomous or learning systems make it difficult to attribute responsibility for outcomes (Matthias, 2004). One response is to ask whether responsibility can remain with designers, users, owners, institutions, or collective actors despite diminished predictability or direct control. Responsibility Continuity does not claim that all responsibility gaps can be eliminated. Instead, it identifies a common structural pathway through which gaps become more severe: the loss of connection among the relied-upon information, the actual judgment, the decision authority, the action, and the subject capable of response.

The idea of a moral crumple zone adds another dimension. Elish (2019) shows how the nearest visible human may absorb blame when a complex automated system fails, even if that human had limited control over the system’s design or operating conditions. Responsibility Continuity rejects this compression of system responsibility into the final human touchpoint. It requires the relation among upstream design, organizational authorization, interface constraints, operator judgment, and corrective authority to remain visible. The point is not to remove responsibility from operators, but to prevent visibility from being mistaken for control.

## 3.6 Algorithmic accountability and reviewability

Algorithmic accountability is broader than model transparency. It concerns who must justify, monitor, contest, and remedy algorithmic systems. Kroll et al. (2017) argue that accountability can be supported through procedural and technical mechanisms even when full transparency is impossible. Nissenbaum (1996) earlier identified barriers to accountability in computerized society, including many hands, opacity, and the treatment of computers as causal agents. Cooper et al. (2022) emphasize accountability as relational: it depends on the relations among actors, institutions, practices, and those affected.

Reviewability similarly shifts attention from isolated output explanation to the socio-technical decision process. Cobbe et al. (2021) argue that automated decisions should be reviewable across the system lifecycle, including the organizational and human processes surrounding an output. Responsibility Continuity shares this process orientation. Its specific contribution is to identify continuity as the condition that allows reviewability to remain meaningful after repeated replication and transformation. Review cannot reconstruct what has become structurally disconnected. If the version, interpretation, judgment, and responsible subject are no longer related, reviewability exists only in form.

## 3.7 The specific contribution of Responsibility Continuity

The adjacent fields can be summarized without collapsing them. Provenance preserves informational history. Data lineage preserves derivation. Traceability preserves observable paths. Explainability supports understanding of model behavior or outputs. Human oversight creates a role for intervention. Meaningful human control connects system behavior to human reasons and authority. Responsibility-gap analysis examines failures of attribution. Reviewability supports institutional examination. Algorithmic accountability asks who must justify and remedy system effects.

Responsibility Continuity asks whether these informational, human, institutional, and temporal relations remain connected from the version relied upon through interpretation and judgment to decision, action, and an answerable subject. It is a relational concept, not a file property; a temporal condition, not a one-time approval; and an institutional requirement, not a claim that a single person must control every technical step. Its focus is not the mere existence of records or humans, but the continued intelligibility of the relation by which an output acquires practical force and remains open to correction.


# 5. Structural Conditions of High-Replication AI Systems

High replication is not merely the production of more content. It is the low-cost and repeated transformation of information through generation, rewriting, summarization, translation, recombination, migration, and downstream use. A proposition developed in one context can become a business recommendation, a technical description can become an instruction, an internal report can become an executive summary, and a model output can be embedded directly in an automated workflow. Each transformation may preserve part of the original content while losing part of the context needed to interpret it responsibly.

High replication becomes governance-relevant when information can proliferate, change form, move across contexts, and influence action faster than institutions can preserve the relations needed for interpretation and responsibility. The concern is not the quantity of copies by itself, but the widening distance between the object in circulation and the judgment, limits, and authority that originally gave it meaning.

High replication should not be confused with scientific reproducibility. Reproducibility asks whether a method or result can be independently reproduced under specified conditions. High replication describes the capacity of content and system outputs to proliferate, mutate, and become operational, often without preserving the conditions under which they were first formed. Nor is high replication identical to scalability. A scalable service can process more users while preserving a stable task and controlled version. High replication emphasizes branching, transformation, and the multiplication of contexts. It is also broader than virality, because a widely shared item may remain informational, while a less visible model output may silently propagate through many automated decisions.

The governance difficulty can be illustrated through a simple hypothetical sequence. A research team publishes a cautiously qualified report. A language model produces a short summary. A second system translates the summary. A vendor converts the translated text into a risk flag. An organization embeds the flag in a prioritization process. A staff member sees only the resulting category and confirms the recommended action. Every stage may appear locally reasonable. Yet the qualifications that constrained the original report may have disappeared before the decision was made. The source remains identifiable, but the relation between the source’s meaning and the final action has broken.

A second case involves time rather than semantic transformation. An institution evaluates and approves version 1 of a system. The vendor later updates the model, changes a default threshold, and replaces the explanatory interface. The institution continues operating under the original approval. Logs show that updates occurred, but no one determines whether the earlier judgment remains valid. Technical continuity exists in the form of a deployment history, while responsibility continuity fails because the current system state is covered by a judgment made about a different object.

A third case involves organizational distance. A platform ranks applicants using a model supplied by a third party. Managers define a policy that treats the ranking as advisory. In practice, staff face time pressure and rarely depart from it. When an adverse outcome is challenged, the vendor says the customer controlled use, management says employees made the final decision, and employees say the system effectively determined the order. The system contains many human and organizational participants, but the relation among influence, authority, and answerability has become incoherent.

These examples show why the relevant unit is not a single model output. The relevant unit is the moving relation among object, context, judgment, authority, action, and consequence. High replication destabilizes this relation because content can change role faster than institutions can reassess responsibility.
This environment complicates source and version relations. In conventional publishing and recordkeeping, a file often moves through a relatively visible sequence from drafting to release to use. In AI-mediated environments, multiple branches can develop simultaneously. The same document may be summarized by one model, translated by another, edited by several teams, and recombined with external data. The resulting outputs may share a source without sharing the same limitations, purpose, or institutional status. A later reviewer may see a revised document and mistakenly assume that an earlier decision was based on the revised content. A system may preserve the final file while losing the version that was actually reviewed and adopted.

High replication also changes semantic function. Summarization removes detail; translation reorganizes emphasis; simplification weakens qualifications; commercial rewriting may increase certainty; and technical implementation may convert descriptive content into executable instruction. The wording may appear substantially similar while the practical force of the content changes. A cautious observation may become a directive; advice for one setting may be treated as a general rule; a research draft may be used as a compliance basis. Semantic movement is therefore not only linguistic transformation. It can change the authority, purpose, and responsibility attached to an output.

Judgment itself becomes distributed. Model developers set objectives, system designers define defaults, managers determine scope, reviewers process individual outputs, and operators carry out actions. Distributed judgment is not inherently defective; complex organizations require specialization. The problem arises when a system cannot distinguish between technical processing and choices that materially alter action. Ranking may be described as information organization while determining who receives attention. A default may be called a technical configuration while shaping resource allocation. A summary may be treated as neutral compression while removing a critical limitation. Consequential judgment can therefore be hidden inside apparently technical steps.

High replication further expands organizational distance. An output may originate in one company’s model, be modified by another platform, integrated by a contractor, and deployed by a separate institution. Each participant may hold only partial knowledge and accept only a narrow contractual role. More participants do not necessarily create more accountability. They may instead create the assumption that someone else has already verified, authorized, or accepted the relevant risk. The whole system can become under-accountable even while each local actor appears to have completed a defined task.

Errors also propagate differently. An undetected error can enter many versions, languages, and downstream systems, eventually losing its visible connection to the original defect. Correcting the source does not automatically correct derivative copies. Old versions may remain active, decisions may continue to rely on them, and institutions may be unable to identify which actions were affected. Cross-language and cross-jurisdictional movement further changes meaning, legal expectations, and responsibility assumptions. A recommendation in one institutional culture may be interpreted as a requirement in another; a research reference may become operational authority; an internal statement may be treated as a public commitment.

These conditions produce five related breaks. A **source break** occurs when an output loses a reliable connection to its canonical source or original context. A **version break** occurs when the system cannot establish which version was reviewed, authorized, or used. A **semantic break** occurs when wording persists but meaning, limitation, purpose, or institutional force changes. A **judgment break** occurs when no one can identify who handled the relevant uncertainty, conflict, exception, or choice. A **responsibility break** occurs when action has produced consequences but no actor or institution remains clearly answerable. These breaks reinforce one another. High replication is not itself the problem; the problem is increasing replication without a corresponding capacity to preserve the relations on which accountable action depends.

# 5. The Capabilities and Limits of File Integrity and Provenance

File integrity and provenance are essential foundations of Responsibility Continuity, but they are not responsibility itself. File-integrity mechanisms answer whether a technical object has changed. Hashes, version identifiers, timestamps, and related records can help distinguish files and releases, detect modification, and stabilize the object under review. Provenance has a wider scope: it represents where an entity came from, which activities affected it, which agents participated, and how records relate over time. W3C PROV provides an interoperable vocabulary for such relations and explicitly treats provenance as information about entities, activities, and agents involved in producing a thing (W3C, 2013a, 2013b).

Integrity records solve an object-identification problem. They help establish which file is being discussed and whether it differs from an earlier state. This is valuable for research papers, software, project records, public works, organizational documents, and long-term archives. Yet an unchanged file can be understood in different ways and used for different purposes. A research discussion can later be treated as operational advice; a draft can be mistaken for an official position; an analysis with limitations can be converted into a definitive conclusion. Technical sameness does not guarantee semantic, contextual, or responsibility continuity.

A hash does not prove authorship, originality, legal ownership, factual truth, ethical legitimacy, or decision correctness. It can support an evidentiary record by showing that a specific file existed in a particular state, but it cannot independently exclude earlier sources, collaboration, licensed material, third-party content, or competing rights. A false statement can be perfectly preserved. An unsuitable recommendation can be accurately traced. A formal version can remain contestable. Treating verifiability as truth gives technical form an authority it does not possess.

Canonical status must likewise be separated from correctness. A canonical version is a stable public reference within a publication system. It enables citation, comparison, and revision to focus on a defined object. It does not mean that the object is infallible or suitable for every later context. Timestamps and publication dates establish that a claim or version existed no later than a particular date, but they do not by themselves prove absolute historical priority across all languages, regions, archives, and unpublished work. Public records can establish documented continuity without supporting an unlimited claim of global firstness.

Provenance also does not establish why an output was adopted, who interpreted it, which limitations were understood, who possessed authority, or who accepted the consequences. It answers where an object came from and how it changed; Responsibility Continuity asks how that object entered judgment and action. The original author may be responsible for a work’s content without being responsible for every downstream use. A distributor may be responsible for representation without controlling final decisions. A user may be responsible for action while lacking information about upstream transformation. These roles cannot be resolved by provenance alone.

The relation can be stated simply: integrity preserves object state; provenance preserves object history; judgment determines practical meaning; responsibility connects decision to consequence. None of these functions should be substituted for another. Provenance is a necessary but insufficient condition for accountability. Without provenance, responsibility lacks a stable factual basis. With provenance alone, responsibility may still lack an identifiable judgment-bearing subject.

# 6. Responsibility Continuity

**Responsibility Continuity**, as formally introduced and developed in this paper by Xufen Tu, is the maintained, visible, and interpretable connection among the source and version relied upon, the human interpretation and judgment involved, the consequential decision made, the action that followed, and the person or institution that remains accountable for the result. It rejects the idea that responsibility can be fully represented by a signature, a confirmation click, an approval field, or a single moment at the end of a workflow. Responsibility is a relation that must remain intelligible across information formation, version change, semantic transformation, decision, implementation, and consequence.

The first relation is **version relation**. A consequential decision should be traceable to the informational state on which it was actually based, including the content, limitations, revision status, and context visible at the time. Preserving only the final file can distort later review. The second is **interpretive relation**. Information must be assigned practical meaning before it can support action. The same document can be treated as research, warning, advice, policy, or instruction. Interpretation determines which facts are considered relevant, which limitations are accepted, and which institutional context is applied. The third is **judgment relation**. Judgment addresses uncertainty, conflict, exception, purpose, and acceptable consequence. It connects what is known to what should be done. If no one can identify who accepted a risk, resolved a conflict, or decided to proceed, responsibility will migrate toward the model, the process, or the final operator.

The fourth relation is **decision relation**. Judgment must produce an identifiable direction. Decisions may appear as approvals, refusals, parameter choices, continued operation, failure to stop, acceptance of a model recommendation, or maintenance of a default. In complex systems, consequential decisions are often embedded in configurations, rankings, thresholds, and permissions rather than expressed as formal commands. The fifth is **action relation**. Decisions acquire real-world effect through human or automated execution. Responsibility Continuity does not assign all upstream responsibility to the final executor, but it requires the relation between execution and prior judgment to remain understandable. The sixth is **accountability relation**. Someone must remain capable of explaining, correcting, suspending, remedying, or reassessing the result. That subject may be an individual or an institution, but it must possess real authority and continuing obligations rather than only a formal title.

These relations are not a public implementation checklist or a universal data schema. Different industries and institutions will embody them differently. The concept explains what must not become structurally invisible without prescribing any operational mechanism for recording it. Responsibility Continuity is therefore not equivalent to more documentation. A system may contain extensive logs, forms, and approvals while remaining unable to show which version entered judgment, who possessed decision authority, why the action continued, or who can now correct the outcome. Record volume is not responsibility clarity.

Responsibility Continuity is best understood as a **relational condition** rather than an object, record, or chain of custody. A file can possess provenance, a transaction can possess a log, and a model can possess documentation; Responsibility Continuity exists only when these informational elements remain meaningfully connected to judgment and accountable authority. It cannot be stored in a single field because no single field can represent whether the relevant person understood the version, possessed authority, acted for an institution, or remains capable of remedy.

It is also a **dynamic condition**. The relation can be strong at one moment and weak later. A clearly authorized deployment may lose continuity after staff turnover, vendor substitution, model revision, or repurposing. A system may preserve continuity in one domain but not another. For example, technical operators may retain precise version information while affected persons have no access to a responsible institutional contact. Conversely, an institution may provide an appeal office while being unable to reconstruct which model state and data were used. Responsibility Continuity therefore cannot be assessed solely at launch. It concerns the survival of accountable relations across time and transfer.

The concept should not be confused with a simple responsibility chain. A chain suggests a linear handoff from one actor to another. AI systems often involve branching and overlapping forms of responsibility: design responsibility, data responsibility, procurement responsibility, policy responsibility, professional judgment, operational execution, and corrective supervision. Responsibility Continuity permits layered and collective responsibility. Its requirement is not that responsibility be singular, but that the relations among these layers remain intelligible. Collective responsibility becomes problematic when “collective” means that no member or institution can explain the relation between its contribution and the outcome.

Nor is Responsibility Continuity identical to a causal chain. A party may be causally involved without bearing normative responsibility, and a responsible institution may be answerable even when a particular individual did not directly cause the harm. The concept does not derive responsibility from causation alone. It attends to authority, role, knowledge, control, accepted obligation, and the capacity to correct. Causal reconstruction is relevant, but accountable relation is a broader institutional question.

Responsibility Continuity is likewise not an audit trail, although audit trails can support it. An audit trail reconstructs events; Responsibility Continuity asks whether the reconstructed events identify real judgment and answerability. A complete log may record that a reviewer approved an output. It may not show that the reviewer lacked the underlying report, was unable to reject, or was instructed to follow the system. Conversely, meaningful responsibility may sometimes be preserved through a smaller number of well-connected records rather than comprehensive surveillance.

Finally, the concept includes **corrective continuity**. Accountability is not complete when a historical decision-maker can merely be named. A responsible system must identify who can act now: who can suspend, reassess, explain, remedy, or assume the inherited obligation after personnel or institutional change. Corrective continuity turns accountability from retrospective blame into an ongoing capacity for governance.
Responsibility Continuity also differs from nominal human oversight. A person may be present without being informed, empowered, or able to refuse. A reviewer may process too many outputs to exercise genuine judgment. An institution may list a responsible officer who cannot access the relevant model, data, or configuration. Human participation under those conditions can function as ceremonial endorsement or blame absorption. Responsibility should correspond to information access, authority, control, time, competence, and correction capacity. This correspondence echoes the meaningful-human-control requirement that attributed responsibility be commensurate with a human actor’s ability and authority to influence the system (Siebert et al., 2022).

The concept has temporal and cross-system dimensions. A decision may affect people for months or years. Models, data, and configurations change; staff leave; vendors change; platforms migrate. If responsibility exists only at initial approval, long-running systems can retain historical authorization without a current responsible subject. Likewise, an output may move from model to platform to organization to automated action, with each transfer changing permissions and assumptions. Responsibility Continuity requires that transfer, update, and institutional change do not become automatic points of responsibility disappearance.

The concept also distinguishes theoretical authorship continuity from system accountability. This paper’s public records establish how the concept was developed, by whom, and in relation to which prior works. The theory itself concerns how judgment and accountability remain connected within consequential systems. Both involve continuity, but they are not the same claim. Public DOI records, versions, dates, and author metadata preserve scholarly provenance; Responsibility Continuity explains the relation required for accountable action.

# 7. Human Judgment as the Connecting Interface Between Information and Action

AI can generate, classify, compare, predict, optimize, retrieve, simulate, and coordinate. These capacities can exceed the scale and speed of individual human work. Yet expanded capability does not automatically transfer responsibility. AI may become a major infrastructure for information processing and execution without acquiring the authority to determine which ends are legitimate, which harms are acceptable, which values should prevail, or who should answer for the outcome.

There is no fully automatic path from information to action. A probability must be interpreted, a recommendation weighed, a classification placed within an institutional setting, and a forecast converted into a decision to act or refrain. Models can provide relevant information, but information alone does not establish the legitimacy of action. This is the **judgment boundary**: the point at which description, prediction, or recommendation begins to shape material consequence. At that boundary, the question is not merely whether the system can produce an answer. It is whether the answer is appropriate to the context, whether important limitations are missing, whether the action conflicts with existing rights or duties, and who is authorized to respond.

Many low-risk, reversible, and standardized operations can be automated. Format conversion, repeated calculation, file comparison, routine monitoring, and low-consequence sorting do not require continuous human approval. Responsibility Continuity is not an argument for inserting manual review into every computational step. It becomes most important when systems influence rights, opportunities, professional treatment, institutional eligibility, resources, financial outcomes, or irreversible consequences. In those settings, AI may predict risk but does not thereby acquire the legitimate authority to exclude a person; it may recommend an option but does not independently decide which consequence another person should bear.

Human judgment is structurally necessary not because humans are infallible but because responsibility requires a subject capable of understanding reasons, accepting challenge, revising a decision, and responding to consequences. Humans can be biased, negligent, uninformed, and mistaken. The point is not human superiority. It is the difference between producing an output and occupying a social, legal, professional, or institutional position of answerability. A model can generate an apology or an explanation-like text; it cannot lose a professional license, fulfill a public duty, accept institutional sanction, compensate a harmed person, or bear a continuing moral obligation in the way a human or human institution can.

A meaningful human judgment node therefore requires more than presence. It requires adequate information, real authority, sufficient time, an effective capacity to refuse or suspend, and continuing correction duties. A reviewer who cannot see the version used cannot judge it. An operator who can only approve cannot exercise substantive authority. A formal right to refuse that is punished or rendered impossible by workflow design is not an effective right. A person asked to review unmanageable volumes cannot serve as a reliable responsibility boundary. Institutions that assign responsibility without providing the corresponding knowledge, time, access, and power create another form of responsibility transfer.

Human judgment also performs conflict arbitration, exception handling, and refusal. Complex systems must balance efficiency, safety, fairness, privacy, cost, innovation, and institutional purpose. These objectives may not be jointly maximizable. Models can display trade-offs, but the prioritization of values expresses an institutional and normative position. Rules operate on general patterns, while particular cases may require recognition of exceptions. Most importantly, a real responsibility node must be able to say no: to reject a recommendation, pause an automated process, demand additional information, or choose not to act. A human who can only move the system forward does not exercise complete control.

The future human role should therefore not be defined as the residue of tasks machines cannot yet perform. Such a definition shrinks as technology advances. A more durable role lies in interpreting purpose, setting boundaries, resolving value conflict, recognizing unacceptable consequences, authorizing or refusing action, and bearing responsibility. The most important future human node may not process the most information or press the final button. It is the node that connects meaning, authority, decision, and consequence.

# 8. From Provenance Records to Responsible Action

The path from provenance to responsible action begins with source and canonical version. Source identifies where information was formed, who released it, and under what status it entered a public or institutional environment. Canonical version establishes which file functions as the formal reference among drafts, variants, translations, and derivative copies. Neither source nor canonical status proves truth, but both provide the stable background necessary for review. A decision cannot be examined accurately if the actual version relied upon is unknown.

Integrity and version records then establish the object state. They support comparison, identify alteration, and reduce disputes about which file existed at a relevant moment. Yet a technical record cannot bridge the distance from document to action. That distance is filled by interpretation and judgment. A file does not determine how it should be understood, whether it applies to a situation, which qualifications matter, or whether it provides a sufficient basis for action. The consequential transition occurs when a human or institution treats the information as a reason to proceed, modify, refuse, or maintain a course of action.

A decision may take the form of active authorization or consequential inaction. Failure to stop, failure to update, maintenance of a default, and continued use of an old version can all have decision effects. Action then implements the direction through a person, platform, device, or automated process. Automated execution changes the location of responsibility but does not erase it. The relevant questions include who established the execution conditions, accepted the scope, retained suspension power, and remains obligated to address unexpected outcomes.

The continuous structure can therefore be stated as follows: source identifies origin; canonical version identifies the formal reference; integrity establishes object state; interpretation places content in context; judgment addresses uncertainty and conflict; decision selects direction; action implements direction; accountability identifies who must explain, correct, and respond. No single document, signature, hash, model card, approval click, or log entry can substitute for the whole relation. Reviewability scholarship similarly argues that accountability must address the socio-technical decision process before, during, and after an individual automated decision rather than focus only on a model output or explanation (Cobbe et al., 2021).

This principle also governs the paper’s own publication structure. The author’s name, title, version, date, prior DOI publications, final DOI, language relationships, website, and file-integrity records together preserve the public provenance of the work. The concept’s principal scholarly source will be the final published English edition and its DOI. The website functions as a public identity and research hub, while private repositories preserve internal development without becoming required public evidence. This arrangement demonstrates a limited form of the distinction the paper advocates: a public theory can be verifiable and citable without disclosing private operational methods.

# 9. Responsibility Drift in Distributed Systems

High-replication AI systems are rarely produced or governed by a single model, organization, or actor. An output may begin in a foundation model, pass through a specialized service, enter an enterprise platform, be shaped by internal rules, receive human review, and finally trigger automated or manual action. Each layer can add a transformation while narrowing its own account of responsibility. Developers focus on technical performance and say use belongs to deployers; platforms describe outputs as recommendations and say decisions belong to users; organizations rely on policy and say final judgment belongs to staff; staff follow the system and say the direction came from elsewhere.

Responsibility drift is the progressive weakening of a clear relation between influence, authority, control, and accountability. It may occur across contracts, organizational boundaries, model updates, data supply chains, interface design, defaults, and workflow pressures. A party with little formal liability may control a decisive parameter. A party bearing formal responsibility may lack access to the upstream system. A user may see a single “AI result” even though the result combines model output, platform ranking, organizational rules, and human acceptance. The phrase “the AI decided” compresses these separate choices into an apparently unitary technical cause.

Data and updates create additional drift. Data providers may describe themselves as supplying material; developers may assume the data was appropriately governed; deployers may assume model outputs include necessary validation. If limitations disappear downstream, no participant may accept responsibility for the resulting effect. A system may also be approved in one version and later updated in ways that alter behavior, purpose, or risk. Continued use without renewed judgment allows historical approval to cover a technical state that was never actually assessed.

Responsibility drift is temporal as well as organizational. Original developers, managers, and reviewers leave. New personnel inherit a running system without inheriting the reasons, limitations, or authority relationships that justified it. If an organization preserves access credentials but not judgment context, the system becomes a historical artifact no current actor can explain. Responsibility Continuity therefore requires not only initial authorization but institutional succession: someone must inherit oversight, correction, and answerability.

Cross-language and cross-jurisdictional use further change responsibility. Translation can alter certainty, authority, and the distinction between recommendation and requirement. Automatic translation can create the assumption that linguistic accuracy is the platform’s responsibility, while the original author disclaims translated meaning and the user assumes the system has guaranteed equivalence. Legal and institutional expectations also change across jurisdictions. A research reference in one context may be treated as a formal basis in another. Technical portability does not create automatic continuity of authority.

Scale pressure can turn human review into a moral crumple zone. Organizations may retain an approval step while assigning volumes that make substantive judgment impossible. The human is then visible enough to absorb blame but not sufficiently informed or empowered to alter outcomes. Responsibility may drift upward, when operators blame systems and policy; downward, when designers and managers assign all responsibility to the final user; or outward, when all participants attribute the action to an algorithm or automated process. Responsibility Continuity does not require all responsibility to be centralized. It requires the multiple layers to remain connected so that upstream design, platform configuration, organizational authorization, professional judgment, execution, and correction duties can be distinguished and reviewed.


# 10. Cross-Language, Cross-Context, and Semantic Continuity

High-replication AI systems often operate across languages, jurisdictions, professions, and organizational cultures. These transfers are not neutral. A text can preserve its apparent topic while changing certainty, authority, intended audience, or practical function. Responsibility Continuity therefore includes a semantic and contextual dimension: the relation between the meaning originally judged and the meaning later acted upon must remain sufficiently intelligible.

## 10.1 Translation as a responsibility event

Translation is commonly treated as a transmission service. In consequential contexts, it is also an interpretive event. Languages divide uncertainty, obligation, recommendation, and authority differently. Terms such as “may,” “should,” “shall,” “risk,” “recommendation,” “approval,” and “responsible” carry institutional meanings that are not always preserved by literal substitution. A translation can make a cautious statement appear categorical or make a formal requirement appear optional.

Automatic translation increases scale but can weaken ownership of the resulting meaning. The original author may disclaim the translation, the platform may describe it as automated, and the user may assume that the system has guaranteed semantic equivalence. Responsibility Continuity requires a clearer relation: what is the status of the translated version, who has confirmed its use for the relevant context, and which version governs if the texts diverge?

An author-verified semantic edition differs from an automatic translation. It represents a new act of judgment in which the author confirms that the target-language text preserves the concept, argument, boundary, and citation relation of the source. It may use different sentence structures and examples because semantic alignment is not word-for-word identity. The author’s responsibility applies to the verified edition, not to every unauthorized or automatically generated translation.

## 10.2 Contextual transfer

Context changes can be as important as language changes. A statement written for research discussion may be used in a business workflow. A model card intended for technical users may be shown to the public. A voluntary recommendation may be embedded in a mandatory process. The content may remain unchanged while its institutional force changes.

Responsibility Continuity treats such transfer as a potential new decision point. The original judgment established that the content was appropriate for one purpose. A new purpose may alter the affected population, the consequences, the applicable standards, or the authority required. The earlier judgment cannot automatically authorize the new use. Someone must decide that the transfer is appropriate and accept responsibility for the changed function.

## 10.3 Jurisdictional transfer

AI services can cross borders more easily than legal and professional authority. A system developed under one regulatory framework may be deployed in another with different privacy rules, anti-discrimination duties, professional standards, appeal rights, or public-law constraints. Technical portability does not preserve legal legitimacy.

Responsibility Continuity does not determine which jurisdiction governs. It requires that jurisdictional transfer not be treated as a purely technical deployment. The responsible institution must identify which duties now apply, which actor possesses local authority, and who can answer to affected persons. An output cannot carry its original responsibility structure across borders by default.

## 10.4 Semantic compression and authority inflation

Summaries, dashboards, labels, and rankings compress information. Compression is often necessary, but it can inflate authority by removing the uncertainty and reasoning that made the original output appropriately cautious. A long analysis becomes “high risk.” A conditional recommendation becomes “not eligible.” A probabilistic forecast becomes a traffic-light category. The user encounters a simple decision object whose apparent certainty exceeds the source.

Responsibility Continuity requires attention to what was removed and who accepted the compressed form as a sufficient basis for action. The issue is not that every user must read the full source. It is that some responsible subject must understand the relation between the compressed representation and the underlying uncertainty, and that corrective review must be able to reach the fuller basis when necessary.

## 10.5 Semantic continuity and disagreement

Semantic continuity does not require perfect agreement among interpreters. Complex concepts can support reasonable disagreement. The requirement is that disagreement occur around identifiable versions and acknowledged interpretive choices. If two institutions rely on the same text but apply different standards, Responsibility Continuity does not force one interpretation. It requires each institution to own and explain the judgment by which its interpretation became action.

This point prevents the concept from becoming a demand for semantic uniformity. Continuity preserves relation, not sameness. It allows translations, adaptations, and contextual uses to differ, provided that the difference is visible, authorized, and connected to a responsible subject.


# 12. Fields of Application

In research and scholarly publishing, Responsibility Continuity concerns the relation among authorship, AI assistance, version status, canonical publication, citation, and correction. AI can assist with language, structure, search, code, and summarization, but the author remains responsible for deciding which claims to retain, verifying references, stabilizing definitions, authorizing release, and correcting error. A version number and DOI provide stable references but do not prove the truth of the argument. Citation responsibility requires that a cited claim actually appear in the cited version. Automatically generated references should not be accepted because they look formally complete.

In enterprise governance, AI is used for market analysis, customer communication, risk evaluation, hiring support, supplier screening, internal search, and strategy. Responsibility Continuity requires a distinction between system recommendation and organizational decision. Technical teams may select and configure a system, business units may use it, management may define policy, and staff may execute actions. The final employee should not bear responsibility for upstream choices beyond that employee’s authority, while those controlling goals, budgets, scope, and deployment should not externalize responsibility onto downstream operators. Third-party systems make this relation especially important because contractual allocation may not match actual control.

In public administration, automated systems may affect eligibility, resource distribution, public safety, prioritization, and institutional opportunity. Public bodies cannot transfer public responsibility to a vendor or an opaque technical process. Human oversight must connect to an institution with authority to review evidence, recognize exceptions, correct error, and respond to affected persons. Article 14 of the EU AI Act requires human oversight measures for high-risk systems and emphasizes the ability of human overseers to understand capabilities and limitations, remain aware of automation bias, interpret outputs, disregard or override them, and interrupt the system where appropriate (European Union, 2024). These requirements do not exhaust Responsibility Continuity, but they demonstrate the policy importance of substantive rather than nominal oversight.

In education, the relevant relation is not simply how much AI was used but whether students understand, select, and can explain submitted work, whether teachers preserve professional judgment, and whether institutions allocate responsibility coherently. Schools cannot encourage extensive AI use while placing all resulting uncertainty on individual students or teachers. In professional services such as law, medicine, finance, and engineering, AI may support research and pattern recognition but cannot become the final unaccountable source of professional action. The professional and institution must understand the relevant version and limitations, while responsibility should not be imposed on an individual for technical systems that the individual cannot access, control, or correct.

In software and automated systems, mature version control does not by itself preserve decision responsibility. A repository may show what changed without showing why a critical configuration was accepted, who assessed the risk, or who authorized deployment. AI-generated code may run while containing unsuitable assumptions or vulnerabilities. Technical traceability must connect to requirements, authority, deployment, monitoring, and correction. In archives and cultural records, Responsibility Continuity requires clear distinctions among original records, digitized copies, restorations, translations, edited versions, and generated reconstructions. Different versions may coexist, but their status and transformation should not be obscured.


## 10.1 Research publishing and scholarly continuity

Research publishing makes the distinction between object continuity and responsibility continuity particularly visible. A manuscript may exist as an early draft, a repository file, a preprint, a translated edition, a revised PDF, and a version of record. Each can be technically authentic while expressing a different stage of author judgment. A later reader who cites an early draft as the final position may preserve file provenance while misrepresenting scholarly responsibility. Conversely, an author may revise a claim without preserving the earlier version, making it difficult to reconstruct which proposition was publicly available at a particular time.

AI assistance adds another layer. The central question is not whether a model produced individual sentences, but whether the author understood, selected, verified, organized, and accepted responsibility for the published argument. A model can propose a citation that does not exist, compress a qualification into a stronger claim, or make two language versions appear equivalent when a conceptual distinction has shifted. Responsibility Continuity in publishing therefore connects the cited source, manuscript version, author judgment, release authorization, and continuing correction duty. It does not require disclosure of every private drafting interaction. It requires that the public work have a stable version, a responsible author, and a reconstructable relationship to the sources and prior works on which it relies.

A useful boundary case is a paper whose final PDF is unchanged but whose public description is rewritten by an automated platform. The abstract shown to readers may overstate the conclusion. The file integrity of the paper remains intact, yet the semantic and responsibility relation surrounding its discovery has changed. Responsibility Continuity asks who controls that description, whether the author can correct it, and which representation a later user actually relied upon.

## 10.2 Enterprise decisions and organizational authority

In enterprises, AI outputs rarely act alone. They are embedded in budgets, performance expectations, interface design, procurement decisions, policy, and managerial authority. Consider a supplier-screening tool that ranks vendors. The platform provider may define the model, procurement leadership may require its use, analysts may review the scores, and executives may approve contracts. If a supplier is excluded, the last analyst may be visible, but the analyst may not control the data, threshold, or policy. Responsibility Continuity requires the organization to preserve the relation among those layers rather than treating the final confirmation as the sole decision.

Another case involves informal conversion from advice to rule. A company may initially describe an AI recommendation as optional. Over time, employees learn that departures require lengthy justification, while acceptance is automatic. No formal policy changes, but the system becomes functionally mandatory. The nominal decision remains human; the effective decision migrates to the technical default. This is a responsibility-continuity problem because formal authority and practical control no longer align.

Enterprise continuity also depends on institutional memory. A system may be introduced by one leadership team, configured by a consultant, and inherited by managers who know only that it is “approved.” Vendor documents remain available, but the internal reason for accepting the system, the limits originally agreed upon, and the person authorized to change it have disappeared. A responsible organization must preserve more than procurement history. It must preserve enough judgment context to determine whether continued use remains justified.

## 10.3 Public administration and public answerability

Public administration adds legal authority and public duty. When AI supports eligibility, prioritization, fraud detection, inspection, or resource allocation, the affected person does not encounter an abstract model; the person encounters the state or a public institution. Outsourcing technical development does not outsource public responsibility. A vendor may explain system performance, but only the authorized institution can justify why that performance was considered an acceptable basis for exercising public power.

A formally available appeal is insufficient if the appeal body cannot access the relevant version, reconstruct the basis of the decision, or alter the outcome. Responsibility Continuity requires the appeal relation to reach an institution with real corrective authority. Otherwise, the system provides procedural appearance without substantive review. Article 14 of the EU AI Act is relevant because it emphasizes the ability of human overseers to understand limitations, detect over-reliance, interpret outputs, override them, and interrupt operation. These capacities matter not simply at the interface but throughout the institutional path by which an output acquires public force.

Public-sector systems also demonstrate why a single responsible person may be inappropriate. Responsibility may properly belong to a public body, professional office, or multi-level administrative structure. What matters is that institutional responsibility does not become an abstraction. An affected person should be able to identify which body made the decision, which rules and system state were used, and which body can now provide correction.

## 10.4 Education, learning, and authorship

Education reveals the difference between generated performance and human understanding. A student may submit a fluent essay generated through AI assistance while being unable to explain the argument or verify the sources. The file has an author name, but the educational judgment concerns what the student knows and can defend. Responsibility Continuity connects the sources used, the student’s interpretive work, the final submission, the evaluation decision, and the institution’s rules. It cannot be reduced to an AI-detection score.

Teachers and institutions face parallel responsibilities. A teacher may use AI to draft feedback or grade suggestions. If the teacher reviews the output meaningfully, understands the evidence, and retains the ability to depart from it, AI remains an aid. If workload causes automated comments or scores to be accepted without judgment, professional responsibility has shifted while formal authorship remains with the teacher. Schools that encourage AI use but provide no stable rules, appeal processes, or training create responsibility drift among student, teacher, vendor, and administration.

Educational continuity also matters across time. A learner may depend on generated summaries that obscure foundational gaps. Short-term performance appears successful, while later courses reveal that the learner never formed the judgment needed to apply knowledge independently. Responsibility Continuity in education therefore concerns not only document provenance but the continued relation between tool use, human understanding, assessment, and institutional responsibility for learning conditions.

## 10.5 Professional and high-consequence services

In medicine, law, finance, engineering, and similar fields, professional responsibility is not created by the fluency of an output. A model may identify patterns or retrieve relevant materials, but the professional must determine whether the output applies to the specific case, whether contrary evidence exists, and whether the proposed action meets professional duties. At the same time, an individual professional should not absorb unlimited responsibility for a system mandated by an institution and controlled by a vendor.

Consider a clinical support system updated after hospital approval. The new version changes how risk is displayed, while clinicians are not informed that the threshold has changed. A clinician relies on the interface, a patient is affected, and the hospital points to final clinical judgment. The phrase “clinical judgment” cannot resolve the case if the clinician lacked meaningful information about the technical change. Responsibility Continuity requires the relation among vendor update, institutional validation, professional interpretation, and patient consequence to remain visible.

In legal or financial services, generated text may appear authoritative while containing invented authority, outdated law, or inappropriate assumptions. The professional remains responsible for verification, but the organization also bears responsibility for training, system selection, workload, and correction. The concept does not prescribe a universal allocation. It prevents the allocation question from being answered solely by pointing to the last human who touched the output.

## 10.6 Software, automation, and infrastructure

Software projects already use repositories, issue trackers, tests, logs, and release records, yet these do not automatically preserve decision context. A commit shows that a configuration changed. It may not show why a risk was accepted, who had authority to approve deployment, or whether the change altered the basis of prior authorization. AI-generated code intensifies this gap because code can be produced rapidly and integrated by developers who understand local behavior but not all assumptions.

Infrastructure systems also persist beyond their creators. A model or automated process may continue operating after the original team disbands. New maintainers inherit code and permissions but not the judgment that defined acceptable use. A purely technical handover can therefore preserve functionality while losing responsibility. Responsibility Continuity requires institutional inheritance of supervision and correction, not just transfer of credentials.

Incident response provides a clear test. After harm occurs, can the organization identify the deployed version, the change that affected behavior, the people and institutions that accepted the risk, and the current party able to stop or remedy the system? If the answer is no despite complete technical logs, the failure is not simply traceability. It is a failure of continuity between trace and responsibility.

## 10.7 Archives, cultural records, and long-term public memory

Digital archives increasingly contain originals, scans, restored images, colorized versions, translations, edited compilations, and AI-generated reconstructions. All may have legitimate value, but their status should not be merged. A generated completion of a damaged image is not the same object as the source artifact. A translation can preserve meaning while altering interpretive emphasis. A restoration can improve visibility while introducing choices that later viewers mistake for original features.

Responsibility Continuity in archives connects the source object, transformation history, curatorial judgment, public presentation, and the institution responsible for correction. It does not demand one authoritative interpretation of culture. It demands that the boundary between source, reconstruction, and interpretation remain visible. When material is later used in education, commerce, or public narrative, the institutional force of the representation may change, requiring renewed responsibility rather than mere preservation of the original file record.

## 10.8 Cross-domain structure

Across these fields, the common question is whether the system can still explain what was relied upon, how it was interpreted, who made the consequential choice, how action followed, and who remains responsible. Responsibility Continuity is not a universal solution to bias, error, illegality, or institutional failure. It is a structural condition that makes meaningful review, correction, and learning possible.

# 12. Provenance Tool as a Limited Public Demonstration

The **Provenance Tool** associated with this research (Tu, 2026f) is not a complete implementation of Responsibility Continuity and does not automatically determine responsibility. It is a limited public demonstration of how file state, version relation, verification, and comparison can provide a technical basis for later human judgment. Its public functions include creating a record of a file or project state, verifying whether a present object corresponds to an earlier record, and comparing records or versions. These functions address object identity, version, and change; they do not establish truth, authorship, originality, legal ownership, ethical legitimacy, decision quality, or final accountability.

This boundary is central to the tool’s credibility. Technologies that generate hashes and timestamps are sometimes described as proving originality or protecting ownership. Such claims exceed what the records establish. A record can indicate that a specific object existed in a particular form at a certain time. It cannot independently exclude prior sources, determine rights, or establish that the content is accurate or appropriate. The tool can support research archives, project delivery, document comparison, and public release records while leaving legal, professional, factual, and institutional judgments to the relevant human and institutional processes.

The tool’s relation to the theory is therefore limited but meaningful. Responsibility Continuity depends on a stable object and version relation; otherwise, later reviewers may not know what was judged or acted upon. The tool demonstrates part of this lower informational layer. It does not implement the higher relations of interpretation, authority, consequential decision, action, or answerability. It should not be presented as an automated originality system, a rights adjudicator, or a complete AI-governance platform.

The paper, tool, and website consequently have different roles. The final paper and DOI will define and stabilize the theory. The tool’s public version will document its limited software functions. The website will link authorship, papers, authorized editions, and public metadata. Private repositories may preserve development history and non-public methods. Keeping these roles distinct prevents any single technical or publication object from being assigned authority beyond its capacity.

# 13. Boundaries, Limitations, and Non-Claims

Responsibility Continuity cannot guarantee that information is true, decisions are correct, humans are unbiased, or institutions are legitimate. A document may have a clear source, stable version, and valid integrity record while containing error or prejudice. A visible human decision-maker can still be negligent, conflicted, uninformed, or mistaken. The concept preserves the conditions under which responsibility can be examined; it does not predetermine the result of that examination.

The concept does not prove authorship, originality, or legal ownership. Public dates, version records, author declarations, DOI metadata, and integrity records can support documented continuity and show that a work existed publicly no later than a given date. They do not by themselves exclude prior sources, joint work, licensed material, or competing legal claims. This paper therefore makes a verifiable continuity claim rather than an unlimited historical-priority claim. It states who formally introduced and systematized this specific concept and structural relation in the documented research record; it does not claim that every adjacent term or idea originated here.

Responsibility Continuity is not a universal legal standard. Legal responsibility varies by jurisdiction, field, contract, professional duty, and fact pattern. The theory can help identify who possessed information, authority, control, and correction capacity, but it cannot replace courts, regulators, professional bodies, or legislative judgment. Nor does it provide medical, legal, financial, engineering, or educational advice. Applications in those fields illustrate structural relevance, not professional guidance.

The concept does not require human approval for every automated operation. Routine, reversible, low-risk, and standardized functions may be automated. Human review applied indiscriminately can create fatigue and obscure the decisions that truly require judgment. Responsibility Continuity focuses on consequential boundaries and does not disclose or prescribe a universal risk-scoring method for identifying them. Specific institutions must develop proportionate arrangements under applicable law, professional standards, and context.

The theory does not require unlimited record retention or total public transparency. Excessive records can create privacy, security, surveillance, and management risks without improving understanding. Some information properly remains confidential, privately reviewable, or access-controlled. The requirement is that key responsibility relations remain available to the appropriate reviewing subjects, not that every internal discussion be permanently public.

The paper is conceptual rather than empirically comprehensive. Its applications are illustrative and require future case studies, comparative research, institutional analysis, and operational evaluation. Responsibility Continuity overlaps with meaningful human control, responsibility-gap analysis, traceability, reviewability, provenance, algorithmic accountability, and socio-technical governance. Its contribution should be assessed through dialogue with those fields rather than by ignoring them. Future researchers may test, criticize, narrow, extend, or replace parts of the concept. Accurate citation preserves the relation between the original definition and later changes; it does not restrict theoretical development.

# 14. Discussion: From Human-in-the-Loop to Human Responsibility

Human-in-the-loop language remains useful, but it can conceal more than it reveals. A human may appear in a workflow without possessing the information, authority, time, or refusal capacity required for judgment. A signature may occur after a decision has become irreversible. A reviewer may be asked to confirm outputs at a scale that makes substantive review impossible. Responsibility Continuity shifts the question from whether a human is present to whether human judgment remains meaningfully connected to the information, decision, action, and consequence.

This shift also reframes the relation between capability and legitimacy. AI may outperform individuals in prediction, search, pattern recognition, and optimization without acquiring the right to determine which values should prevail or who should bear a consequence. Technical capacity answers what a system can do. Responsibility asks who is authorized to decide whether it should do so and who must respond if the outcome is harmful. NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework similarly emphasizes that trustworthy AI involves governance, accountability, transparency, human roles, and context-sensitive risk management rather than model performance alone (NIST, 2023, 2024).

As AI becomes infrastructure, embedded choices can become invisible. Ranking, recommendation, refusal, and allocation may appear as natural properties of a technical environment rather than results of objectives, defaults, data, design, and institutional policy. Governance must therefore address enduring structures, not only individual outputs. Initial approval is insufficient for systems that update, migrate, expand in purpose, or change their decision role. Historical authorization cannot indefinitely cover future technical states. Version change must remain connected to renewed interpretation and, where consequences materially change, renewed judgment.

Responsibility Continuity is also a power relation. The ability to transform an output into action, decide another person’s opportunity, allocate resources, or allow a system to continue is a form of authority. Responsibility and power should be broadly aligned. Organizations should not retain benefits while externalizing system risk to users or frontline staff. Public institutions should not avoid public duties by invoking vendors. Professionals should not allow fluent model language to substitute for professional judgment. Upstream designers should not treat downstream use as eliminating all relevance of design choices.

Yet the concept neither individualizes all responsibility nor makes “the organization” an empty answer. Complex consequences may involve design, policy, procurement, professional judgment, execution, and supervision. Responsibility Continuity helps distinguish these levels and preserve their relation. It avoids both the scapegoating of the nearest human and the abstraction of responsibility into a system no one can answer for.

The theory also changes the meaning of transparency and explanation. More information is not automatically more accountable. Technical disclosure may be incomprehensible, irrelevant, or unsafe. A fluent explanation may rationalize an outcome without reflecting the actual basis of the decision. Accountability requires an intelligible relation among the version used, reasons considered, authority exercised, action taken, and person or institution capable of correction. Provenance preserves a contestable object; judgment records preserve a contestable decision; responsibility identifies a subject who can be challenged.

Responsibility Continuity is therefore not only a blame structure. It is a learning structure. Stable source and version relations help locate error; judgment relations help identify what was missed; responsibility relations identify who can stop harm, repair outcomes, and revise practice. Without these connections, organizations may search for a scapegoat or repeat failures they cannot reconstruct. The concept seeks to move AI governance from output management toward relationship governance.

## 13.1 Institutional memory

Institutional memory is often treated as a documentation problem, but it is also a responsibility problem. Organizations preserve contracts, logs, and technical records while losing the reasons that made a system acceptable. Staff turnover separates present operators from past judgment. A new team may know that a system was approved without knowing which limitations were material, which uses were excluded, or who had authority to revisit the decision. Responsibility Continuity requires that enough judgment context survive for current actors to determine whether inherited authority remains valid.

This requirement does not mean preserving every conversation. Institutional memory should be proportionate and role-sensitive. What must survive is the relation necessary to reconstruct consequential authority: which system state was approved, for what purpose, under which assumptions, by which responsible institution, and with what continuing correction duty. Without this relation, historical approval becomes an orphaned artifact that legitimizes present operation without present judgment.

Institutional memory also includes responsibility succession. When the original decision-maker leaves, the obligation does not vanish. An institution must identify who inherits the duty to monitor, reassess, and respond. Succession is especially important for systems embedded in infrastructure, where the absence of a current responsible subject may remain unnoticed until a failure occurs.

## 13.2 Corrective capacity

Accountability is sometimes understood as the ability to assign blame after harm. Responsibility Continuity treats corrective capacity as equally important. A responsible relation must reach a subject who can do something: suspend a system, revise a policy, reopen a decision, notify affected persons, repair a record, provide remedy, or commission a new judgment. Naming a historical actor without identifying present corrective authority creates retrospective visibility but not governance.

Corrective capacity also distinguishes meaningful appeal from symbolic appeal. An appeal channel that cannot access the relevant evidence, change the outcome, or reach the responsible institution does not restore continuity. The same is true of technical incident reporting. A report can document failure while no party has authority to change the deployed system. Continuity is restored only when information about the failure connects to actionable responsibility.

Correction should not be understood only as reversal. Some harms cannot be fully reversed. Corrective responsibility may involve explanation, mitigation, compensation, institutional learning, or prevention of recurrence. The appropriate response varies by domain, but the structural need remains: consequence must connect to a subject with an ongoing obligation to respond.

## 13.3 Power and responsibility

Responsibility Continuity is inseparable from power. The ability to determine which output enters action, which person is prioritized, which resource is withheld, or which process continues is a form of authority even when expressed through technical settings. Systems often distribute this power without naming it. A threshold, ranking rule, interface default, or procurement choice can shape outcomes more strongly than the final human confirmation.

Responsibility should broadly correspond to this distribution of power. This does not imply strict equality between causal influence and liability. It means that subjects with significant authority over purpose, design, deployment, and continuation should not disappear from the responsibility account merely because another person executed the final step. Equally, frontline users should not be treated as passive whenever they possess real discretion and knowingly accept a consequential direction. Continuity allows these distinctions to be made without collapsing the system into either total individual blame or total institutional abstraction.

Power also affects the reality of refusal. A worker may formally possess override authority while facing organizational penalties for using it. A public officer may have discretion while being evaluated against automated throughput targets. A professional may be told that a model is only advisory while deviations trigger audit. Responsibility Continuity must therefore attend to effective authority, not merely written authority. The social and organizational conditions under which judgment can be exercised are part of the responsibility relation.

## 13.4 Continuity as a condition of contestability

A system is contestable when affected persons and reviewing institutions can challenge its operation or outcome in a meaningful way. Contestability requires more than an explanation interface. A challenge must reach the relevant version, reasons, authority, and corrective subject. Without continuity, a person may receive fragments: a model explanation without the policy decision, a policy statement without the model version, or a contact person without authority.

Responsibility Continuity supports contestability by preserving the object and subject of challenge. It enables a claimant to ask not only “Why did the model produce this output?” but also “Why was this version used, who decided it was sufficient, what role did the institution assign to it, and who can now reconsider the result?” These are governance questions that no single technical explanation can answer.

## 13.5 From output governance to relationship governance

Many AI-governance practices concentrate on outputs: accuracy, bias, explanations, prohibited content, or performance. These remain important, but high-replication systems can replace one output while preserving the same broken responsibility relation. A model can become more accurate while institutional authority remains unclear. A new explanation can be added while no one can correct a decision. A provenance record can be improved while human review remains ceremonial.

Relationship governance shifts attention to the connections that make outputs consequential. It asks whether the relevant source and version are known, whether interpretation and judgment are real, whether authority and control align, whether action can be stopped, and whether responsibility survives transfer and time. Responsibility Continuity names this relational requirement. It does not displace output governance; it explains why output governance alone cannot sustain accountability.


# 15. Objections, Counterarguments, and Further Theoretical Clarification

A concept intended for broad use in AI governance should be tested against plausible objections. Responsibility Continuity may be criticized as too demanding, too human-centered, too difficult to operationalize, or insufficiently distinct from existing ideas. These objections are important because they clarify the boundaries of the claim and prevent the concept from becoming a vague label for every form of accountability.

## 14.1 Objection: existing provenance and audit systems are already enough

One objection is that modern systems already maintain extensive logs, model versions, access records, approvals, and incident histories. If these records can reconstruct what happened, a separate concept of Responsibility Continuity may seem unnecessary. The response is that reconstruction of events does not necessarily reconstruct judgment or answerability. Logs may show that a person approved an output without showing what that person saw, whether the person understood the limitations, whether refusal was possible, or whether the person remains able to correct the result. An audit system can preserve chronology while obscuring authority.

The distinction can be illustrated by two organizations with identical technical records. In the first, reviewers receive the full relevant context, possess time and authority to challenge the recommendation, and can trigger reconsideration after deployment. In the second, reviewers receive only a summary, face penalties for disagreement, and cannot change the system after approval. The log structure may be the same. The responsibility relation is not. Responsibility Continuity describes the difference that technical reconstruction alone cannot capture.

## 14.2 Objection: responsibility can be assigned by law or contract

A second objection is that responsibility need not be structurally continuous because law, regulation, or contract can assign it after the fact. A provider, deployer, employer, public authority, or professional can be designated as legally responsible regardless of the internal decision process. Formal allocation is indispensable, but it does not eliminate the need for continuity.

First, legal responsibility and practical corrective capacity may diverge. A party may be formally liable but unable to reconstruct the decision or stop the system. Second, contracts can allocate risk between parties without creating a responsible relation for affected persons. Third, legal proceedings often depend on evidence about knowledge, authority, causation, reasonableness, and control. Responsibility Continuity does not replace legal attribution; it preserves the relations that make attribution and remedy more informed.

The concept also helps identify situations in which formal responsibility is unfairly assigned. A frontline employee may be designated as the final decision-maker while lacking meaningful discretion. A professional may be held to account for an institutional system that changed without notice. A contract may describe a platform as a neutral tool even though its defaults materially structure outcomes. Continuity analysis reveals these misalignments without predetermining the legal result.

## 14.3 Objection: the concept places too much emphasis on humans

A third objection is that Responsibility Continuity is excessively anthropocentric. Future AI systems may possess greater analytical capacity, stable memory, self-monitoring, and sophisticated correction functions. Why insist that responsibility remain connected to humans or human institutions?

The answer depends on the meaning of responsibility in this paper. Responsibility is not merely the ability to detect error or modify behavior. It includes legitimate authority, social obligation, institutional duty, contestability, and the capacity to bear consequences within a human normative order. A system may become better than a person at identifying mistakes while still operating under goals, permissions, and institutional conditions established by human society. The relevant question is not whether AI can perform correction, but who has authorized the system to define and enact correction and who answers to those affected.

This claim need not assume that current legal and moral categories will never change. If future institutions recognize new forms of artificial agency, the theory may require revision. Under present conditions, however, moral, legal, professional, and public responsibility remains organized through human persons and human institutions. Responsibility Continuity reflects that institutional reality while remaining open to later theoretical challenge.

## 14.4 Objection: continuity is impossible in complex systems

Complex systems involve thousands of actors, components, updates, and interactions. Demanding continuity among source, version, judgment, action, and accountability may appear unrealistic. No institution can preserve every relation or identify every contribution.

The objection is correct if continuity is interpreted as exhaustive documentation or total causal knowledge. That is not the claim. Responsibility Continuity is proportional and consequential. It concerns the relations necessary to understand and respond to decisions with material effects. Not every low-risk transformation requires the same degree of preservation. Nor must every contributor become a final responsibility node. The requirement is that complexity not be used to erase the key relation by which authority entered action.

Complex systems already make selective judgments about what must be preserved: safety-critical changes, financial authorizations, clinical decisions, access controls, and legal approvals. Responsibility Continuity proposes that the judgment and accountability relations surrounding consequential AI use deserve similar institutional attention. The precise form will vary, but the impossibility of total documentation does not justify total discontinuity.

## 14.5 Objection: the concept is too difficult to measure

A concept that cannot be reduced to a score may seem difficult to apply. Responsibility Continuity includes qualitative relations such as understanding, authority, refusal, and answerability. These cannot always be measured directly, and attempts to convert them into metrics may create false precision.

This limitation is real, but it is not unique. Legitimacy, due process, professional judgment, organizational responsibility, and meaningful consent also resist complete numerical representation while remaining essential governance concepts. Responsibility Continuity is therefore presented as a theoretical relation rather than an assessment instrument.

This paper does not publish a scoring model, measurement formula, implementation template, operational checklist, or automated method for assigning responsibility. Those matters remain outside the public scope of this paper.

## 14.6 Objection: organizations need speed, not additional friction

Organizations may argue that preserving responsibility relations slows innovation and undermines the benefits of automation. High-replication systems are valuable precisely because they reduce the cost and delay of producing and acting on information. Additional review can become bureaucratic, especially when consequences are uncertain.

Responsibility Continuity does not require uniform friction. It distinguishes routine and reversible functions from consequential boundaries. The appropriate question is not whether every action should slow down, but whether the system knows where speed creates irreversible or institutionally significant effects. Failure to preserve responsibility can itself create severe friction later: disputes, failed audits, inability to correct, repeated harm, reputational loss, or legal uncertainty. Continuity can therefore be understood as preserving the organizational capacity to move quickly without becoming unable to explain or repair what has been done.

The earlier principle of Judgment Before Momentum is relevant here. Momentum is not rejected. It is ordered. Speed should follow sufficient judgment at the points where action acquires serious consequence, while routine processes remain capable of automation.

## 14.7 Objection: authorship continuity is self-protective rather than theoretical

Because this paper discusses its own authorship, DOI, versions, and public research record, readers may view its continuity statements as self-protective claims unrelated to the theory. The paper must therefore distinguish scholarly provenance from theoretical proof. Public records do not establish the truth of Responsibility Continuity or exempt it from criticism. They establish only the documented relation among author, version, definition, and prior work.

This distinction is itself theoretically relevant. A concept can be responsibly criticized only when its source and version are stable enough to identify what is being criticized. Scholarly provenance preserves a contestable object. It does not create an untouchable authority. The author-continuity statement is therefore not offered as evidence that the concept is correct; it is offered to prevent the concept’s formal definition and research lineage from being erased or confused during high-replication dissemination.

## 14.8 Further theoretical implications

The objections reveal several deeper implications. First, Responsibility Continuity is a condition of **institutional intelligibility**. An institution must be able to understand its own consequential actions, not merely possess records of them. Second, it is a condition of **responsible delegation**. Delegation to systems, vendors, teams, or professionals remains legitimate only when authority and answerability survive the delegation. Third, it is a condition of **correction across time**. A system that cannot identify a present corrective subject is not fully accountable even if its historical origin is known.

Fourth, Responsibility Continuity is a condition of **bounded automation**. Automation becomes institutionally bounded not only through prohibitions, but through maintained relations to human judgment and correction. Fifth, it is a condition of **public trust without blind trust**. Trustworthy systems do not ask users to believe that technical records guarantee correctness. They make it possible to identify who decided, on what basis, under what authority, and with what continuing duty.

These implications position Responsibility Continuity as neither a technical standard nor a moral slogan. It is a conceptual bridge between informational provenance and institutional accountability. Its usefulness will depend on whether future research can test the concept across domains, identify where continuity fails, and distinguish meaningful continuity from merely formal compliance.


# 16. Conclusion

AI is becoming a major infrastructure for generation, analysis, coordination, and execution. Its expanded capacity does not automatically create expanded responsibility. The central governance problem in high-replication systems is not only whether an output can be traced, but whether the relation among version, interpretation, judgment, decision, action, and accountability remains visible after repeated transformation and distributed use.

Responsibility Continuity, introduced and systematized in this paper by Xufen Tu, describes that relation. It treats responsibility not as a final signature or isolated approval, but as a continuous structure linking source, canonical version, integrity record, human interpretation, human judgment, consequential decision, implementation, and accountable response. It extends a research line from Judgment Before Momentum through the irreducible role of human judgment, the Human Responsibility Node, and Responsibility Drift. The resulting claim is that high-replication systems must preserve not only technical objects but also the human relation through which those objects acquire practical force.

Provenance and file integrity are necessary foundations. They establish the object and its history. They do not prove truth, authorship, ownership, legitimacy, or correctness, and they cannot decide who understood, authorized, or accepted a consequence. Human judgment must likewise be substantive rather than ceremonial. A person without relevant information, time, authority, refusal power, or correction capacity is not a meaningful responsibility node simply because the person appears in a process.

The future human role should not be defined as the residual set of tasks machines cannot yet perform. A more durable role lies in interpreting purpose, setting boundaries, resolving value conflict, recognizing unacceptable consequence, authorizing or refusing action, and answering for outcomes. This role may be exercised by individuals or accountable human institutions. It does not require all responsibility to be concentrated in one person, but it requires distributed responsibilities to remain connected rather than drift into an unanswerable technical abstraction.

The paper’s claim is deliberately limited. Responsibility Continuity does not guarantee correctness, eliminate bias, replace law, or provide a complete implementation method. It establishes a structural condition for accountability. Responsible AI depends not only on whether machine outputs can be tracked, but on whether information can still be connected to judgment, judgment to decision, decision to action, and action to a human or human institution that can be identified, questioned, corrected, and held answerable.
